# Example 3 — When the skill tells you you're wrong

**Skill:** `executive-presence` · **Mode:** spoken 1:1 · **Stakes:** relationship

Shows the coach **declining the requested deliverable**. The user asked for help making a case. They get told to ask a question instead — and then get the case anyway, for if listening doesn't resolve it.

---

## The ask

> There's a new director on the data platform team who keeps rejecting our schema change requests. Third one this quarter. My team is frustrated and wants me to escalate to my VP. I'm meeting her one on one next week. I want to make the case that their review process is blocking us.

---

## The response

### Diagnosis

You're preparing to argue a position you've built entirely from your side of three rejections. You know what she decided; you don't know why. That's a thin basis for a case, and it's a very thin basis for an escalation.

Worth naming directly: a new director rejecting three schema changes in a quarter is behaving like someone who has found something — inherited data quality problems, an incident you didn't see, a compliance constraint. That's at least as likely as an obstructive review process, and you currently can't tell the difference. Walk in with a prepared argument and you'll find out which it was in the worst possible way.

### Frameworks

- **Read Before You Respond** — primary, and it's the actual recommendation here, not a warm-up. You're one question away from information that could change your entire position.
- **A·D·A** for when you *do* advocate, and specifically for telling the difference between updating (she has a real constraint) and caving (she pushed back and you folded).
- **C·D·S** to make the cost concrete if the conversation gets that far.

Notably **not** a Minto-structured case. Structuring an argument is the wrong prep for this meeting.

### The words — how to open

> "I wanted to talk about the schema review process, but before I give you my read on it — what are you seeing from your side? Three of our changes have come back this quarter and I want to make sure I understand what you're protecting before I push on it."

Then be quiet and actually listen. Do not have your rebuttal loaded while she talks.

**When she answers, name it back before you respond:**

> "So the concern is [her stated reason]. Is that the core of it, or is there more?"

That second question surfaces more than the first one does, roughly every time.

### The words — if there's a real constraint on her side

Then the meeting isn't about her process, it's about your shared throughput:

> "That makes sense, and it's not something I had visibility into. So the real problem is [her constraint] meeting [your need] — can we design something that handles both? For example [a pre-review checkpoint / a fast lane for additive-only changes / a standing 30 minutes each week]."

### The words — if the process genuinely is the problem

Only if listening hasn't produced a good reason. Now make the case, with the cost quantified:

> "I hear the review standard and I'm not asking you to lower it. Here's my constraint: three changes have been in review for [N] weeks total, which has held [specific deliverable] and pushed [dependent work]. **[C·D·S]**
>
> I need a path where a straightforward additive change clears in [target time]. I'd rather build that with you than route around it — what would you need in a request for that to be safe?"

### Delivery note

Pause after your opening question and **do not fill the silence.** This is the single highest-value pause in the conversation. If she takes five seconds to think, that's five seconds of her deciding how candid to be — talking into that gap costs you the honest version of her answer.

Slow down noticeably on "I'd rather build that with you than route around it." That's the line doing relationship work, and rushed, it sounds like a threat rather than a preference.

### Coaching note

On the escalation your team wants: escalating a peer conflict you haven't tried to resolve directly tends to damage your standing more than theirs, because it tells your VP you couldn't handle a peer conversation. If you do escalate later, "I met with her, here's what I learned, here's what we couldn't solve" is a strong position. "She keeps blocking us" is not.

---

## What to notice

The user asked for a case. They got told to ask a question — but they were still equipped with the case, for the branch where listening doesn't resolve it. That's the difference between a recommendation and a hedge.

This isn't the model being agreeable. It's [Framework 10](../skills/executive-presence/references/frameworks.md#10-read-before-you-respond) as a deliberate instruction: *"Someone who walks into a contested room with a prepared argument and no curiosity usually loses the room."*
