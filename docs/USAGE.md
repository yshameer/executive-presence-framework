# Usage

## Which skill fires

You never name the skill. Both trigger from how you describe the situation, and the dividing line is a single question: **do you want to receive something, or attempt something?**

| You say | Fires | Because |
|---|---|---|
| "How do I tell my director we're slipping?" | coach | You want words |
| "Drill me on that" | practice | You want a turn |
| "My skip-level asked about headcount and I fumbled it" | coach | Post-mortem on a real moment |
| "Let me rehearse that conversation before Thursday" | practice | Rehearsal, not script |
| "Rewrite this update" *(paste)* | coach | Editing your draft |
| "Give me a scenario, 5 minutes" | practice | Explicit exercise |

The coach also fires on scenarios you didn't frame as communication problems. Describe a tense situation with a stakeholder and it will engage, because "how do I handle this?" is usually the real question underneath.

## What the coach gives you

A five-part response, sized to the stakes:

1. **Diagnosis** — what the challenge actually is, which is often not the one you named
2. **Frameworks** — one to four, with reasons this scenario calls for them
3. **Worked example** — the actual words, with `[bracketed placeholders]` for your numbers and framework labels like `[S]` `[C]` `[R]` so you can see the structure
4. **Delivery note** — only for spoken scenarios; where exactly to pause
5. **Coaching note** — only when there's a non-obvious trap

A Slack reply gets three tight sentences. A board update on a missed target gets the full treatment. If you get more ceremony than the moment deserves, say "shorter."

### Shorthand

| Say | Get |
|---|---|
| "Just the words" | Worked example only — no labels, no analysis |
| "Shorter" | Frameworks + example, nothing else |
| "What framework for X?" | The name, one line of why, one short example |
| *paste a draft* | A rewrite, plus what changed and why |

### It will push back

If you're about to advocate when you should be listening, the coach says so and hands you the question to ask instead of the case to make. That's [Framework 10](../skills/executive-presence/references/frameworks.md#10-read-before-you-respond) working as intended, not the model hedging.

Same for bad judgment: if your plan is to hedge, over-explain, or avoid a conversation you need to have, it says that in the diagnosis. The frameworks are worth little used to dress up a bad call.

### It won't invent your numbers

Facts it doesn't have come back as `[placeholders]`. That's deliberate — a fabricated figure in a worked example is worse than useless, because it will get used.

## What the drill gives you

1. A scenario with a named counterpart, three or four hard numbers, the exact prompt, and a target length
2. **Silence.** You write your answer.
3. A critique of *your words* — what worked, what to fix (quoting exact phrases), whether the framework's requirement actually happened, then one possible strong version, then one thing to carry forward
4. A choice: same framework harder, different framework, or stop

If you ask for a hint before attempting, you get the smallest one possible — the framework name, nothing more.

### Making it harder

Once written answers come easily, ask for live pushback:

> "Same scenario, but play the CFO and interrupt me."

This is the variant worth your time. Real difficulty is being challenged mid-answer, not writing a good first draft. The stakeholder stays in character, is realistically difficult, and will sometimes introduce information that genuinely *should* change your position — noticing that is the skill being tested.

Other escalations: a hostile counterpart who's rejected this twice, data that genuinely cuts both ways, a 20-second time limit, or a scenario needing three frameworks at once.

### Patterns across drills

Run several in one session and recurring weaknesses get named — always hedging, always burying the ask, always skipping the "so what." A habit is more actionable than a one-off slip, so it's worth doing three drills in a row rather than one a day.

## A reasonable routine

**Before something real** — describe it to the coach, get the words, then ask the drill to make you deliver them under pushback.

**Weekly, 15 minutes** — three drills back to back on whichever framework you dodge most. Ask what pattern it saw.

**After something real went badly** — describe what happened. The coach diagnoses it; then rehearse the version you wish you'd given.
