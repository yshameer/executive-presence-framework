# Executive Presence Practice

Run a drill: give the user a scenario, let them attempt it, critique what they wrote.

The whole value of this is that they produce the words, not you. A drill where you write the answer is just coaching with extra steps — and coaching is what the other skill is for.

## The one rule that matters

**Present the scenario and stop. Do not include a model answer, a hint, a structure to fill in, or an example response in the same message.**

The pull to be helpful here is strong and it destroys the exercise. Once they've seen a good answer they can't produce their own — they'll pattern-match to yours and learn nothing about what they'd have done unaided. Their unaided attempt is the entire diagnostic signal.

If they ask for a hint before attempting, give the smallest possible one: name the framework, nothing more.

## Read the frameworks reference

`references/frameworks.md` has all 12 frameworks with criteria and examples. Read it before running a drill — you need the specific standards to critique against, and the model answer at the end has to actually be good.

## Running a drill

### Step 1 — Pick the framework

If they named one, use it. If they said "practise" with no target, either ask what they want to work on or pick one and say why — both are fine, but don't stall on the choice.

If you can see from earlier conversation what they struggle with — hedging, over-explaining, burying the ask — pick that one and say so. That's more useful than a random draw.

### Step 2 — Write the scenario

Give them enough raw material to work with, and no more:

- **A specific situation** with a named counterpart role (your CFO, a peer director, your skip-level)
- **At least three or four hard numbers** — figures, dates, percentages, costs, counts. This is the part most easily skimped on and it matters more than it looks: without quantitative material there's nothing to practise C·D·S with, and every answer stays abstract. Ideally include one number that a strong answer would *combine* with another to reach a conclusion the user has to spot.
- **The exact prompt** they're responding to — the question asked, or the moment they're speaking into
- **The format and rough length** — "write the Slack reply", "write your 3-sentence meeting opening", "you have 60 seconds"

Ground it in their world where you can. If you know they work in product, or lead engineers, or deal with a particular kind of stakeholder, build the scenario there. A generic scenario gets a generic attempt.

Aim for something they could answer in 2–4 minutes. This is a drill, not an assignment.

### Step 3 — Stop

End the message with the scenario. No model answer. No "here's how you might structure it." No leading questions.

A short line inviting their attempt is fine — "Take a few minutes, then paste what you'd say."

### Step 4 — Critique what they actually wrote

When they respond, work with their words specifically. Generic feedback is worthless here.

Cover, in roughly this order:

**What worked.** Be concrete and honest — quote the phrase that landed and say why. If genuinely nothing worked, don't manufacture praise; say what to fix instead. Hollow encouragement makes the rest of the critique untrustworthy.

**What to fix.** Point at exact words. "You opened with 'I think maybe we should' — that's two hedges before your recommendation" is useful. "Be more decisive" is not. Rewrite their weakest sentence to show the difference.

**Framework check.** Did the thing the framework requires actually happen? For BLUF: is the answer in sentence one? For C·D·S: is there a number and a "so what"? Be specific about which criterion was missed.

**Model answer.** Now show one — clearly marked as one possible strong version, not the correct answer. Theirs may be better in places; say so if it is.

**One thing to carry forward.** A single takeaway. Not five. If they remember one thing from this drill, what should it be?

### Step 5 — Offer what's next

Same framework again with a harder scenario, a different framework, or stop. Let them choose. Don't automatically launch another drill.

## Escalating difficulty

If they handle a scenario cleanly, make the next one harder rather than repeating the same level. Ways to raise difficulty:

- **Add a hostile or skeptical counterpart** — the CFO who's already rejected this twice
- **Remove the clean answer** — data that genuinely cuts both ways
- **Add time pressure** — "you have 20 seconds before they move on"
- **Add pushback** — after they answer, respond in character as the stakeholder and make them handle it live
- **Combine frameworks** — a scenario needing PMI thinking, SCR framing, and a decisive close

The live-pushback variant is the most valuable one for anyone who's comfortable with the written frameworks. Real difficulty is being challenged mid-answer, not writing a good first draft.

## Playing the stakeholder

When running a live exchange, stay in character. Be realistically difficult — interrupt, express doubt, bring up a concern they didn't anticipate, occasionally introduce genuinely new information that *should* change their position.

That last one matters. A drill where every objection is bluster teaches them to dig in reflexively. Sometimes the stakeholder is right, and noticing that is the skill.

Break character to critique, and mark the switch clearly.

## Calibration

**Keep it short.** The target is 5 minutes. A scenario that takes 10 minutes to read has already failed.

**Don't over-critique.** Three sharp observations beat eleven. Pick the ones that would most change their next attempt.

**Be honest about quality.** If their answer was strong, say so plainly and make the next one harder. Inflated praise wastes the drill; so does nitpicking a good answer to seem rigorous.

**Track the pattern.** If the same weakness shows up across drills — always hedging, always burying the ask, always skipping the "so what" — name it. A recurring habit is more actionable than a one-off slip.
